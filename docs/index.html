What the public GitHub event feed is made of

RepoRadar ingests GitHub's public event stream into a partitioned Parquet lake and measures what is in it. This page reports one measurement: the feed is overwhelmingly automated, and the base rate already answers the question a classifier was going to be built for.

Every figure below is re-derived from the lake each time this page is built — 15,827,495 events, 2026-07-22 to 2026-07-29, 96 hour partitions. Nothing here is typed in by hand.

What it ingested

GH Archive publishes one file per hour of GitHub's public event stream. backfill and archive-serve download those hours, convert each into a Parquet partition, and record it in an hours ledger; verify checks the ledger against the files in both directions, and repair-lake re-fetches whatever it proved untrue.

A separate live path exists — a /events poller writing hourly capture files and publishing to Kafka, a validating consumer storing events in TimescaleDB — and none of the figures on this page come from it. They come from complete archive hours, because a share needs a denominator, and the poller's window is exactly the thing this project does not claim to know the size of.

The finding

93.90% of the events in this lake come from repositories with exactly one distinct actor (14,861,750 of 15,827,488), across 1,627,870 of 1,723,968 repositories.

Restrict to the repository-days that carry enough volume to matter, on the day held out of every choice made during the analysis, and the picture sharpens: 95.47% of repository-days with at least 20 events already have exactly one actor.

7 of the 15,827,495 events in this lake carry no repository at all, and are excluded from every per-repository figure — which is why the denominator in the headline above is that many short of the event count in the ingest table. It changes nothing at the precision printed here, and it is stated anyway: a denominator that silently differs from the one two paragraphs up is how a reader loses the ability to check the rest.

That is the result, and it is a negative one about method: there is nothing here worth classifying, because the base rate already answers the question. Anyone consuming this feed can discard the automated bulk with a counter and no model. The public event stream is not a population containing automation — it is automation, carrying a trace of everything else.

What this figure is not

A share of events is a claim about volume, not about population. Read as population it is badly wrong in the flattering direction, and the next section is what that looks like when it happens. The per-repository-day figures are the ones that carry the claim.

The label is a proxy, and it is stated as one

“Automated” here means: a repository-day with exactly one distinct actor and at least 20 events. There is no ground truth for what is a bot, and inventing one would make the exercise circular. Two independently motivated conditions, neither of which is a feature used to predict it — but a human working alone on a busy day is counted as automation, and a bot farm with two accounts is not.

The cheap signal is a volume artifact

The analysis started from a cheap signal: repositories whose name after the slash is five to eight lowercase letters and nothing else — ^[a-z]{5,8}$, the shape that generated repository names tend to take. Measured over events it looks decisive. Measured over repositories it nearly vanishes.

The pattern does not identify a distinct population. It identifies a volume-concentrated one: 13.97% of repository-days carrying 69.81% of events, because a handful of very high-volume repositories carry the sum. Weighting by events makes a weak per-repository signal look overwhelming — those two shares stand 5.00× apart, and reading that gap as separation is how a 1.04× effect gets reported as an order of magnitude.

It also loses on F1, because its recall of 0.8113 drags against a baseline whose recall is 1.0000 by construction. A signal that costs a regex and buys 3.42 points is not a classifier; it is a rounding error with a story attached.

Consequence

No single number answers “how much of the public feed is automated”, and any write-up quoting one is quoting a day. The headline above is quoted as a property of this lake for exactly that reason.

Human attention is tiny, and clean

WatchEvent — someone starring a repository — is 0.033% of everything in the lake: 5,258 events out of 15,827,495. It is also almost perfectly uncontaminated: 99.98% of them land on repository-days outside the automated population. 1 of 5,258 does not.

This is the useful half of a negative result. The bulk of the feed is machine traffic and carries no ranking signal, but the fraction that reflects a person deciding something is small enough to process exhaustively and clean enough to use without filtering.

How this was checked

  • A held-out day. The unit is one repository-day. 2026-07-22, 2026-07-27, 2026-07-28 were available while the threshold, the pattern and the scoring were being chosen; 2026-07-29 was not looked at until they were fixed. Split by calendar order, not at random — the days are not exchangeable, and a random split would let the same repository appear on both sides.
  • A named dumb baseline. Nothing ships here without beating “predict the majority class”. That baseline scores F1 0.9768 on the held-out day, and the signal under test does not beat it.
  • Predictions written down first. Six of them, with an explicit falsifier for each, recorded before any split, threshold or score existed. Two held, three missed, one was void. None was reworded afterwards.

The void one is worth more than the result

The baseline was specified as “predict automated from event count alone, threshold chosen on the training days”, and the label was “exactly one actor and at least 20 events”. The threshold search chose 20 — the label's own constant. The baseline was therefore not competing with the label; it contained half of it, and its recall came back at exactly 1.0000. It could not have failed.

The tell was in the output and nowhere else: an F1 of 0.9768 looks like a strong baseline, while a recall of exactly 1.0000 at a threshold sitting exactly on the label's constant is a disclosure. A metric that cannot fail is not a weak metric — it is a different object, and it is indistinguishable from a good result until you ask what it would print if the claim were false. The re-run makes volume a filter rather than a feature, which is why every score above is measured only among repository-days at or above 20 events.

What was removed, and why

This project used to publish an estimate of how much of the live feed a single poller sees. It does not any more, and the removal is worth more than the number was.

The first attempt reconciled the live capture against the published archive hour covering the same period. On the hours measured, the two sources shared no event identifiers at all, and matching on the commit SHA carried by a push — a value that cannot differ between two records of the same event — found no meaningful overlap in the adjacent hours either. Whatever the cause, the archive could not serve as ground truth for what the poller missed, so the difference would have reported the mismatch rather than the miss. The comparison command survives, and refuses to return a ratio in exactly that case rather than reporting a confident zero.

The second attempt estimated the feed's rate from the live capture alone: events inside a returned page are consecutive, so the spacing between their identifiers is measurable rather than assumed, and the identifiers elapsed between two cycles imply how many events happened in between. That rests on the spacing inside a page describing the spacing outside it. Checked against complete archive hours, it does not — identifiers arrive in dense clusters, neighbours a couple apart inside one and consecutive clusters thousands apart, so measuring inside a cluster and applying it across the gaps prices empty identifier space at the density of a burst.

The estimator was corrected, and the correction changed nothing measurable: a returned page holds about one cluster, so there is no boundary for the two versions to disagree about, and dozens of consecutive cycles produced identical results either way. A residual error of roughly 8.6× against an independently measured event rate remained, and nothing accounted for it. (That residual was measured during the ingestion work; it is not re-derived by this page, whose queries all run against the lake.)

So it was retired rather than re-measured. What the capture path reports now are exact counts — cycles, events fetched, events new — and the question what fraction of GitHub is that? is left open and marked open. A number wrong by an unexplained factor is not a rough version of the right number.

Reproducing this

The lake is not in the repository — it is 1.35 GiB (1,448,310,660 bytes) of public data that the publisher still serves, so it is rebuilt rather than shipped. Everything below runs locally, and needs no account with anyone.

git clone <this repository> && cd reporadar
make setup                                   # uv sync, plus the commit hooks

cp .env.example .env                         # set POSTGRES_PASSWORD, GRAFANA_ADMIN_PASSWORD
make up                                      # Kafka + TimescaleDB, bound to localhost only

reporadar backfill 2026-07-22 2026-07-29     # fetch those days from GH Archive
reporadar verify                             # does the hours ledger match what is on disk?

make site                                    # re-derive every figure above into docs/index.html
make lint test                               # ruff, mypy --strict, pytest

That range names 8 calendar days and this lake holds the 4 of them that were captured; hours the publisher never published are a settled answer rather than an unfinished job, and do not count against the run. make site reads whatever the lake actually contains, so a rebuilt page states the lake in front of it — including a different one. The queries are the whole of tools/build_site.py, and the figures live nowhere else.
